SLIDE 1  Title
KEY POINT: An article pushes on what people think. Physics guesses that push; this work measures it and finds its minimum size.

SCRIPT (about 0.5 min)
Good morning. I'm Brian Mwai, and my dissertation is Measuring the External Field. When you read a news article, it pushes a little on what you think. My question: can we measure how hard it pushes, from the article alone?

DEFINITIONS (if asked)
- Sociophysics: using the tools of statistical physics to model how groups of people behave.

IF ASKED "SO WHAT?"
Opinion models treat media as a push, but everyone guesses how big it is. I built a way to measure a candidate for that push from the content itself. It separates the four groups at 10.7%, with source mixed in, so I don't claim it detects manipulation. And even without knowing the exact strength, the measured spread gives a floor: media would need a coupling of at least 4.29 to flip a group with beta J = 2. That turns a guessed number into a testable one.

SLIDE 2  The idea
KEY POINT: Physics models opinion like magnets. The media's push, h, is always guessed. Can we measure it?

SCRIPT (about 1 min)
Physics models a group of people like a magnet: tiny arrows that line up with their neighbours. Here each arrow is a person, for or against. m, the magnetisation, is the room's overall mood: minus one all against, plus one all for. J, the coupling, is how much people copy each other. Beta, the inverse temperature, is how seriously they take that pressure. And h, the external field, is the media's push on everyone. Studies guess h. My question is whether we can measure it from the article itself.

WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN
- m (magnetisation) = mood of the room, -1 all against to +1 all for
- h (external field) = media's push; general, not magnetic or electric

EQUATION SIDE NOTE
- Ising energy: E = -J sum(s_i s_j) - h sum(s_i). E is the energy of the room; s_i is person i, +1 for or -1 against. Agreeing with neighbours lowers E, and h rewards pointing the media's way.
- Mean-field equation: m = tanh(beta J m + h). Each person feels the room's average mood m instead of each neighbour; tanh keeps m between -1 and +1.

DEFINITIONS (if asked)
- Ising model: a model of magnets where each spin points up or down and prefers to match its neighbours; used here for two-sided opinions.
- Mean field: each person feels the average of the whole group, not particular neighbours; it makes the maths solvable.
- tanh: a smooth S-shaped function that keeps m between -1 and +1.

IF ASKED
Q: What does beta J mean, and why must a be negative?
A: Beta J is how strongly people copy each other. When it's above 1, the room settles into two stable opinions, for or against; in the maths that shows up as a negative coefficient, a. Then a push is needed to flip from one to the other. Alpha itself is positive.

SLIDE 7  Result 1
KEY POINT: The groups really differ: group explains 10.7%, and chance is about 1 in a billion.

SCRIPT (about 1 min)
Yes. I compared the four groups with a one-way ANOVA, the standard test for comparing group averages. It gives three numbers. F, the F-statistic, is the gap between groups divided by the gap within groups: 15.8, where pure chance would give about 1. Eta squared, the share of all variation explained by group, is 0.107, so 10.7 percent. And the p-value, the chance of a result this strong if the groups were really the same, is about one in a billion. A second run gave 8.9 percent. So the groups really differ.

WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN
- F (F-statistic) = 15.779: gap between groups / gap within; chance ~ 1
- Eta squared = 0.107: 10.7% of variation explained by group
- p = 1.0 x 10^-9: about 1 in a billion if the groups were the same

EQUATION SIDE NOTE
- Eta squared: eta^2 = SS_between / SS_total. SS, the sum of squares, adds up squared distances from an average: between the group averages, and in total.
- F-statistic: F = variance between groups / variance within groups = 15.779. If the groups were the same, F would be near 1.

DEFINITIONS (if asked)
- One-way ANOVA: compares the averages of several groups defined by one factor.
- F: spread between group averages divided by spread within groups.
- eta squared: between-group sum of squares over total sum of squares; a share from 0 to 1.
- p-value: the probability of a result at least this large if there were no real difference.

IF ASKED
Q: What is eta squared, physically?
A: It isn't a physical quantity. It's a share between 0 and 1: how much of the total difference between articles is explained by their group. 0.107 means 10.7 percent.

Q: Why a one-way ANOVA?
A: Because there is one thing sorting the articles, the group, and one number per article, the score. That's exactly what a one-way ANOVA tests. I also compared each group with neutral separately.

SLIDE 9  Result 3
KEY POINT: Two runs agree at 0.87 out of 1, and the sign changes are what noise predicts.

SCRIPT (about 0.75 min)
Do the scores repeat? The ICC, the intraclass correlation, measures agreement between two runs: 1 is identical, 0 is none. Ours is 0.87. 51 articles changed sign between runs, and measurement noise predicts 55, anywhere from 44 to 67. So the changes are what noise predicts, and the group averages are reliable.

WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN
- ICC (intraclass correlation) = 0.8725: 1 identical, 0 none
- 51 sign changes; noise predicts 55 (44 to 67)

EQUATION SIDE NOTE
- ICC: intraclass correlation = variance between articles / (variance between articles + run-to-run variance) = 0.8725.
- Noise model: run-to-run noise = standard deviation of (run 1 - run 2) / sqrt(2) = 0.0073. Simulating that noise predicts 55 flips.

DEFINITIONS (if asked)
- ICC: intraclass correlation: agreement between repeated measurements of the same items; 1 is identical.
- Sign reversal: a score positive on one run and negative on the other.

Does the brain model match real brains? I compared it with brain scans of four people watching Friends. r, the correlation, measures how closely two signals move together: 0 is none, 1 is perfect. SLIDE 12  The minimum push
KEY POINT: Media would need a strength of at least 4.29 to flip the room.

SCRIPT (about 1.25 min)
Now the physics. The media's push, h, equals alpha times the score X. Alpha, the coupling constant, is the push per unit of score. I can't measure alpha, so I asked how big it must be to flip the room's majority. The smallest push that does that is the critical field, h c. It depends on beta J, the reduced coupling, which is how strongly people copy each other. At beta J of 2, h c is 0.533. The spread of scores, delta X, is 0.124. So alpha must be at least 0.533 divided by 0.124, which is 4.29. Any claim that these articles flip opinion must assume at least that. It's a minimum, not a claim that media flips opinion.

WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN
- alpha (coupling constant) = push per unit of score
- beta J (reduced coupling) = 2: how strongly people copy; say 'beta J', not 'beta'
- h_c (critical field) = 0.533: smallest push that flips the majority
- alpha >= 0.533 / 0.124 = 4.29

EQUATION SIDE NOTE
- Landau free energy (not the F-test): F(m) = a m^2 + b m^4 - h m, with a = (1 - beta J)/2 and b = 1/12. When beta J > 1, a is negative and two stable opinions (two valleys) appear.
- Critical field: from m = tanh(beta J m + h), h = atanh(m) - beta J m. A valley vanishes where dh/dm = 0, at m*^2 = 1 - 1/(beta J), so h_c = |atanh(m*) - beta J m*| = 0.533 at beta J = 2.
- The bound: the biggest push the articles give is alpha x Delta X. To flip the room it must reach h_c, so alpha >= h_c / Delta X = 0.533 / 0.124 = 4.29.

DEFINITIONS (if asked)
- Critical field: the smallest field that flips the majority.
- Lower bound: a minimum: alpha can't be smaller than this.

IF ASKED "SO WHAT?"
Opinion models treat media as a push, but everyone guesses how big it is. I built a way to measure a candidate for that push from the content itself. It separates the four groups at 10.7%, with source mixed in, so I don't claim it detects manipulation. And even without knowing the exact strength, the measured spread gives a floor: media would need a coupling of at least 4.29 to flip a group with beta J = 2. That turns a guessed number into a testable one.

IF ASKED
Q: Why is this physics?
A: The opinion model is the same maths physics uses for magnets lining up. My part is measuring the push and working out its minimum size.

Q: What does alpha >= 4.29 mean in practice?
A: It's a minimum bar. Anyone who claims these articles flip a group's opinion, where people copy each other at strength 2, must use a media strength of at least 4.29, or the model says it can't happen.

Q: What does beta J mean, and why must a be negative?
A: Beta J is how strongly people copy each other. When it's above 1, the room settles into two stable opinions, for or against; in the maths that shows up as a negative coefficient, a. Then a push is needed to flip from one to the other. Alpha itself is positive.
