The same story can be told calmly in one place and written to alarm you in another. Both shape what we believe. Can we measure how much?

That question became my B.Sc. Physics research at The Catholic University of Eastern Africa.

Physics models public opinion like magnets: people influence each other, and media acts as an outside force on everyone at once. In these models, that force is always guessed. My goal was a first step toward measuring it.

This isn't about calling emotional media fake or propaganda. A true story can still lead with fear. I'm looking at framing: whether content leans on emotion or on reasoning.

I used TRIBE v2, Meta's model that predicts how a typical brain responds to what we read, hear and watch, trained on real brain scans. From it I built a score comparing emotion and reasoning responses, and tested it on 400 news articles.

What we learned:
Fear-driven content stood out most. Outrage stirred emotion and reasoning about equally. The scores held up on a second run.
In the model, media would need a strength of at least 4.29 to flip a tight-knit group's opinion. That's a high bar: small differences in framing aren't enough on their own.

Why it matters: opinion researchers get a number they can test instead of assume, and journalists, educators and public health teams get a way to compare how sources frame stories across hundreds of pieces at once. It rates content, never people.

Next: more sources, audio and video, and real opinion data.
