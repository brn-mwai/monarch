\begin{abstract}
Mean-field opinion models represent media as an external field acting on coupled binary
opinions, and that field is almost never measured. A companion paper shows that a
media-driven transition requires a coupling $\alpha \ge h_c(\beta J)/\Delta X$, where
$\Delta X$ is the attainable range of a content observable. Here we measure one. Text is
converted to speech, aligned at word level and passed to a released encoder that predicts
average-subject fMRI responses on $20{,}484$ cortical vertices; the observable is the mean
predicted activation over an affective-salience parcel set ($1{,}030$ vertices) minus that
over a deliberative-control set ($851$ vertices). No person was scanned. On 400
length-matched items in four categories, each drawn from a single source dataset, the
categories separate at $\eta^2 = 0.1068$ ($F = 15.779$, $p = 1.0\times10^{-9}$) against a
smallest detectable $\eta^2 = 0.0268$. The separation is carried by fear-activating items
(Cohen's $d = 0.94$ against neutral); high-outrage items do not separate ($d = 0.03$)
because they raise both networks almost equally. Because category and source coincide,
the separation is between corpora and cannot be attributed to framing. Against the
source-derived manipulative label the observable reaches $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.627$ while a
TF-IDF baseline reaches $0.976$, so it is offered as a field observable, not a detector. A
second run gives $\mathrm{ICC}(2,1) = 0.8725$ and replicates the separation
($\eta^2 = 0.0888$), but 51 of 400 items change sign, as the session noise predicts, so no
per-item statement is supported. The measured spread $\Delta X = 0.1241$ implies
$\alpha \ge 4.29$ at $\beta J = 2$.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A released cortical encoder turns text into a signed affective-deliberative index
\item Four length-matched corpora separate at eta-squared 0.107 and replicate in a rerun
\item The separation is between source datasets; a TF-IDF baseline far outperforms it
\item High-outrage text raises both networks equally and does not separate from neutral
\item The measured spread 0.124 requires a coupling of at least 4.29 at beta J = 2
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Statistical-physics treatments of opinion formation routinely include a term representing
media. In the mean-field Ising formulation and its relatives \cite{castellano2009,galam2008},
that term is an external field $h$ acting on every agent, and the modelling result is that
beyond a critical magnitude $h_c(\beta J)$ the population is driven from one consensus to
another. The literature on this mechanism is large
\cite{crokidakis2012,azhari2023,freitas2020,sirbu2016} and the qualitative conclusion is
robust within the models.

The field itself is almost never measured. It is assigned, swept, or fitted to an outcome
that the model was built to reproduce, and a recent review of Ising-inspired sociophysics
lists calibration against data among the field's open problems \cite{mullick2025}. A
companion paper \cite{mwai_bound} makes the consequence precise: writing $h = \alpha X$ for
a content observable $X$ with attainable range $\Delta X$, no media-driven transition across
the spinodal of the all-to-all mean-field model is possible unless
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound}
\alpha \ \ge\ \frac{h_c(\beta J)}{\Delta X},
\end{equation}
and this can be evaluated before any data are collected. What it cannot supply is
$\Delta X$, which is an empirical property of a real observable on real content.

This paper supplies one. We build an instrument that computes a candidate observable from
content, apply it to a corpus assembled for the purpose, and report the spread it attains
together with what the measurement does and does not support. Three commitments run through
the work and are stated here so the reader can hold us to them: the physics stays in front
and the encoder is treated as a thermometer rather than a subject; every number is produced
by a released script from released data; and the design's confound is stated before each
result it touches, not deferred.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

Each item is converted to speech, transcribed with word-level timings, embedded as text and
as audio, and passed to TRIBE v2 \cite{tribev2}, a released multimodal encoder, developed
from the TRIBE architecture \cite{tribe2025}, that predicts fMRI responses over
$20{,}484$ vertices of the fsaverage5 cortical surface \cite{fischl2012}. Speech is
synthesised with gTTS \cite{gtts}; when that service is unreachable the encoder's own loader
falls back to the MMS-TTS English model \cite{pratap2024}, so two runs of the pipeline need not
use the same synthesiser. Word timings come from WhisperX \cite{bain2023}, which aligns the
output of Whisper \cite{radford2023}. The encoder embeds text with Llama~3.2-3B
\cite{llama3} and audio with Wav2Vec-BERT~2.0 \cite{seamless2023}. The released checkpoint is
loaded in the average-subject configuration that its loader sets, so every prediction is for
a group-average subject rather than for any individual. The prediction, an array of shape
$(T, 20{,}484)$ over the item's duration, is mean-pooled over time to one value per vertex.
We use the word \emph{scan} for one pass of the encoder over an item or over the corpus;
no person was scanned at any point.

Two parcel sets are taken from the HCP multimodal parcellation (HCP-MMP1.0)
\cite{glasser2016}, bilaterally, and resolved once into fsaverage5 vertex index sets using the
encoder's own atlas utility. The affective-salience set is OFC, pOFC, p24, a24, TGd, TE1a,
TE1p, IFSa, IFSp and AAIC ($1{,}030$ vertices); the deliberative-control set is 46, a9-46v,
p9-46v, 11l, 13l, d32, p32, a10p, p10p and 10pp ($851$ vertices). The sets do not overlap
(Fig.~\ref{fig:roi}). Their assignment to the two labels is the study's specification, made
before the data existed, and is not an independently validated functional division.

\subsubsection{The observable}

Let $A_{\text{aff}}$ and $A_{\text{del}}$ be the mean time-averaged predicted activation over
the affective-salience and deliberative-control vertex sets. The original specification of
this project used a ratio,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NAA}_{\text{ratio}} \;=\; \frac{A_{\text{aff}}}{A_{\text{del}} + \delta},
\qquad \delta = 10^{-3},
\end{equation}
which is a magnitude asymmetry only when both means sit at or above baseline. The encoder's
outputs are unconstrained in sign, and a negative denominator makes the ratio change sign
for reasons that have nothing to do with the content. Across the corpus reported below the
ratio is undefined for 69 of 400 items, $17.25\%$. This is not an edge case to be dropped, so
the analysis is carried by the signed difference
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:naa}
X \;\equiv\; \mathrm{NAA}_{\text{signed}} \;=\; A_{\text{aff}} - A_{\text{del}},
\end{equation}
which is defined everywhere. Positive means affective activation is predicted to lead,
negative means deliberative activation leads. The reformulation changes what the index means:
it reports which set leads, and by how much in the encoder's units, not by what proportion.

The observable is a \emph{cortical proxy}. The released checkpoint predicts activity on the
cortical surface only, so no subcortical structure is measured and none is named. Every value
is a prediction produced by an encoder from content, and the phrase ``predicted activation''
is used throughout in place of any claim about measured brain activity.

\subsection{Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

The corpus is 400 English text items in four categories of 100
(Table~\ref{tab:corpus}). Each item is a passage of whole sentences between 150 and 200
words, so that category is not confounded with the length of the encoder's input. Mean word
counts are $167.4$, $163.5$, $163.7$ and $162.2$ for fear-activating, high-outrage, neutral
and reward-hook items respectively, with standard deviations between $10.2$ and $12.8$.

The category names state the construct each source was chosen to represent; they are not
per-item annotations. Fear-activating items are articles from the ISOT fake-news collection
\cite{ahmed2017} whose title or body contains at least one of 24 threat keywords (for example
``outbreak'', ``attack'', ``toxic''). High-outrage items are articles labelled hyperpartisan in
the SemEval-2019 Task~4 by-publisher collection \cite{kiesel2019}, whose labels are inherited
from the publisher rather than assigned per article. Reward-hook items are the linked-article
bodies of Webis-Clickbait-17 posts judged clickbait \cite{potthast2018}; the teaser itself is
about ten words and too short to be length-matched. Neutral items are a seeded random sample
of English journal-article abstracts from PubMed published 2019--2021 \cite{pubmed}, retrieved
without subject terms, and Reuters wire articles from the ISOT true-news collection. The ISOT
true articles open with a Reuters dateline that the fake articles lack, and a classifier can
separate the two on that string alone; datelines and residual mentions of the agency were
therefore removed before passages were built. The binary \texttt{manipulative} label used in
Section~\ref{sec:auc} is derived from the category (1 for the three non-neutral categories,
0 for neutral), not annotated per item. The working hypothesis, fixed before scanning, was
that the three non-neutral categories would sit above neutral content on $X$, high outrage
most of all.

\textbf{The corpus is not source-balanced, and this is the central caveat of the paper.}
Category and provenance coincide by construction, so any separation the instrument reports is
a separation between corpora and cannot be attributed to framing. Section~\ref{sec:results}
states this alongside the result rather than after it, and Section~\ref{sec:whatsurvives}
identifies which conclusions survive it.

\subsection{Execution and the second run}

The corpus was shuffled with a fixed seed before scanning, so items were scanned
interleaved rather than one category at a time, and each result was appended to the output
as it completed. The first run (A) took four GPU sessions on a Tesla P100. Every row was
checked against the source corpus on text, category and identifier before analysis. A second
run (B) rescanned all 400 items with identical text, identical code and identical parcel
sets. Run A is the primary measurement; run B is used only for reliability and replication.
Neither run is corrected toward the other and the two are not averaged, since an average of
two runs would carry a precision that neither has.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

Separation across the four categories is tested by one-way analysis of variance on $X$,
reporting $F$, its $p$-value and $\eta^2 = SS_{\text{between}}/SS_{\text{total}}$. Each
non-neutral category is compared with neutral content by Welch's unequal-variance $t$-test
\cite{welch1947}, with effect size Cohen's $d = (\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_0)/s_p$, where $s_p$ is
the pooled standard deviation \cite{cohen1988}; with 100 items per group, $s_p^2$ is the mean
of the two sample variances. The same contrasts are repeated on $A_{\text{aff}}$ and
$A_{\text{del}}$ separately. Every $p$-value is the probability, computed under the null
hypothesis of equal means (or no discrimination), of a test statistic at least as extreme as
the one observed; it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Discrimination against the \texttt{manipulative} label is summarised by the area under the
ROC curve, $\mathrm{AUC} = P(X_+ > X_-) + \tfrac12 P(X_+ = X_-)$ for a randomly chosen
labelled-positive item $X_+$ and labelled-negative item $X_-$, which equals the Mann--Whitney
$U$ statistic divided by $n_+ n_-$ \cite{hanley1982,mann1947}. No parameter is fitted for
the observable. Two baselines are scored on the same rows and label: the VADER compound
sentiment score \cite{hutto2014}, as implemented in NLTK \cite{bird2009}, used directly; and
TF-IDF features over unigrams and bigrams (sublinear term frequency, minimum document
frequency 2) with logistic regression, implemented in scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011} and
scored out of fold under five-fold stratified cross-validation. Confidence intervals on AUC
are percentile intervals from $2{,}000$ bootstrap resamples of items \cite{efron1993}. A
source-identity probe fits the same TF-IDF pipeline to predict the source dataset, scored
out of fold.

Test--retest agreement between runs A and B is reported as Pearson $r$ and as the
intraclass correlation ICC(2,1): two-way random effects, absolute agreement, single
measurement \cite{shrout1979,mcgraw1996}. ICC is reported because a run that shifted every
score by a constant would still correlate perfectly. The number of items whose sign of $X$
differs between runs is compared with the number expected under additive, independent
per-run error, estimated from the paired differences (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}).

Sensitivity was computed from the design alone. For the ANOVA, the smallest $\eta^2$
detectable at $\alpha = 0.05$ with power $0.80$ follows from the noncentral $F$
distribution with noncentrality $\lambda = N\eta^2/(1-\eta^2)$ \cite{cohen1988}; for the AUC,
from a seeded simulation of a two-sided Mann--Whitney test under a binormal alternative with
300 positives and 100 negatives. Computations used SciPy \cite{virtanen2020}.

\subsection{Field model}
\label{sec:model}

The field enters the all-to-all mean-field model through the self-consistency condition
$m = \tanh(\beta J m + h)$, with $h$ in units of $k_B T$. For $\beta J > 1$ the spinodal field
at which the metastable branch disappears is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hc}
h_c(\beta J) \;=\; \sqrt{\beta J(\beta J - 1)} \;-\; \operatorname{artanh}\!\sqrt{1 - 1/\beta J},
\end{equation}
which reproduces the values located numerically in the companion paper \cite{mwai_bound} to
the precision quoted here. The spread entering Eq.~\eqref{eq:bound} is the sample range
$\Delta X = \max X - \min X$ over the corpus in run A.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Separation across categories}

The four categories differ at
\begin{equation}
F(3, 396) = 15.779, \qquad p = 1.035\times10^{-9}, \qquad \eta^2 = 0.1068,
\end{equation}
on $n = 400$. The design can detect $\eta^2 = 0.0268$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ and power $0.80$, so
the observed effect is roughly four times the smallest the study could have found.

\emph{Read this with Table~\ref{tab:corpus} in view.} The categories are corpora. The
measurement establishes that the instrument assigns systematically different values to four
groups of documents; it does not establish that framing is what it responds to.

Two features of Table~\ref{tab:welch} deserve emphasis. First, the effect is largely one

shows why, and the answer is not that outrage content leaves the encoder unmoved.

Every category mean is negative, so deliberative activation is predicted to lead on average
in every category (Fig.~\ref{fig:violin}). The instrument finds no affective dominance at
category level anywhere in this corpus.

\label{sec:components}

The observable is a difference, and a difference cannot tell a large symmetric response from
no response. Both components are recorded per item, so each can be compared against neutral
content on its own (Table~\ref{tab:component-welch}, Fig.~\ref{fig:components}).

High outrage raises $A_{\text{aff}}$ by $d = +0.507$ and $A_{\text{del}}$ by $d = +0.491$, both
at $p < 10^{-3}$, and the difference between them by $d = +0.030$. The category that fails to
separate on the observable is the one that moves both networks together. Fear-activating
content raises $A_{\text{aff}}$ by $d = +0.613$ and leaves $A_{\text{del}}$ flat, so its whole
effect on the observable is one-sided. A one-way ANOVA on each component alone gives
$F = 7.451$ for $A_{\text{aff}}$ and $F = 6.399$ for $A_{\text{del}}$, both $p < 10^{-3}$.

This weakens an argument we would otherwise make. If outrage had moved neither component, its
failure to separate would count against the instrument simply detecting provenance. It moves
both, so every category drawn from a distinct source moves away from the neutral baseline on
at least one component, which is what source detection predicts. The components do not
resolve the confound in either direction.

\subsection{Discrimination against the label, and baselines}
\label{sec:auc}

Against the source-derived \texttt{manipulative} label (300 positive, 100 negative), the
observable discriminates at $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.6274$, above the smallest detectable $0.5916$.
Because the label is the category, this is a second view of the separation above rather than
evidence about manipulation in individual items. AUC is the headline because it is
threshold-free. An $F_1$ of $0.6680$ is obtainable, but its threshold was chosen on the same
data it is evaluated on, and it is labelled as fitted in sample wherever it appears.

\textbf{The lexical baseline wins by a wide margin} (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Sentiment
does not clear chance. The observable is not a better detector of manipulative content than
counting words, and no such claim is made. Leave-one-source-out, the natural correction for
the confound, is unavailable because every source contributes exactly one class, so a
held-out source leaves a single-class test fold. The ISOT-only block is the substitute, and
TF-IDF holds at $0.9724$ there. Both TF-IDF figures sit above $0.95$, where near-ceiling
lexical performance on a small corpus points to residual stylistic markers, and a probe on
the same features recovers the source dataset for $66.25\%$ of items against a
majority-class rate of $25\%$. Much of what the lexical model separates is provenance. The
observable is carried forward as the field quantity Eq.~\eqref{eq:bound} consumes, not as a
competing classifier.

\subsection{Run-to-run reliability}
\label{sec:reliability}

Without a second run every effect above would rest on a single pass with no statement of how
much of an item's score is the item and how much is the run. Table~\ref{tab:retest} gives the
agreement between runs A and B.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
& $r$ & ICC(2,1) & $\mathrm{sd}(\Delta)$ & $\mathrm{sd}(\Delta)/\mathrm{sd}_{\text{A}}$ \\
\midrule
$X = \mathrm{NAA}_{\text{signed}}$ & $0.8763$ & $0.8725$ & $0.01029$ & $0.484$ \\
$A_{\text{aff}}$ & $0.8759$ & $0.8713$ & $0.01213$ & $0.489$ \\
$A_{\text{del}}$ & $0.8576$ & $0.8550$ & $0.01271$ & $0.521$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Agreement between two runs over all 400 items. $\Delta$ is the paired difference
B$-$A and $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{A}}$ the between-item standard deviation in run A. ICC is
reported beside $r$ because a run that shifted every score by a constant would still
correlate perfectly.}
\label{tab:retest}
\end{table}

Three consequences follow, and they cut in different directions.

\textbf{The separation replicates.} On run B the four-category ANOVA gives
$\eta^2 = 0.0888$, $F = 12.861$, $p = 4.918\times10^{-8}$, against $\eta^2 = 0.1068$,
$F = 15.779$ for run A over the same items. Both clear the detectable floor. The difference
in $\eta^2$ between runs is $0.018$, with a paired bootstrap $95\%$ interval of
$[-0.014, 0.052]$ ($5{,}000$ resamples of items within category), so the two runs are
consistent with one underlying effect measured twice with error.

\textbf{Per-item direction is not reliable.} Of 400 paired items, 51, or $12.75\%$, reverse
sign between runs. A statement that a particular item is affective-leading is therefore not
supportable at this precision, and no per-item verdict appears anywhere in this paper or
in the public interface described under Data availability. Group means over 100 items average
this scatter down by a factor of ten, which is why the category-level result survives.

\textbf{The reversal count is the measured noise, not an extra defect.} The paired differences
give a per-run error $\sigma = \mathrm{sd}(\Delta)/\sqrt{2} = 0.00728$, against a
between-item standard deviation of $0.01913$ once that error is removed. Under additive error
at that $\sigma$, with each item's value shrunk toward the grand mean so that no item's own
noise predicts its own reversal, $20{,}000$ simulations give $55.3$ expected reversals, $95\%$
interval $[44, 67]$. The observed $51$ sits inside it. Reversing items sit near zero, mean
$|X| = 0.00320$ against $0.02159$ for stable items (Mann--Whitney $p = 1.4\times10^{-25}$),
and spread evenly across categories ($\chi^2 = 2.764$, 3 df, $p = 0.429$). Per-item
direction is therefore out of reach at this $\sigma$, rather than awaiting a fix. Three
sources could contribute to $\sigma$: speech synthesis, which is redone on every run and may
fall back to a different synthesiser; forced alignment; and GPU nondeterminism. Separating
them requires a controlled rerun.

\textbf{Scan order does not carry the separation.} Because run A took more than one GPU
session, position in the scan is a candidate explanation for the category effect. The
interleaved order balances the four categories across thirds of the scan
($\chi^2 = 2.773$, 6 df, $p = 0.837$). A drift across thirds is present, $\eta^2 = 0.0167$
at $p = 0.0353$, but because it is nearly orthogonal to category it adds scatter rather than
bias: removing scan-third means before the category ANOVA moves the effect only from
$\eta^2 = 0.1068$ to $0.1048$, $p = 1.588\times10^{-9}$. Had the corpus been scanned one
category at a time, this drift would have been indistinguishable from the finding.

\subsection{The field: calibration and bound}
\label{sec:physics}

With the observable in hand, the natural step is to estimate $\alpha$ in $h = \alpha X$ by
fitting. We regressed the \texttt{manipulative} label on $X$ (320 items for fitting, 80 held
out) and converted the slope $s$ with the linear-response relation
$m \approx h/(1 - \beta J)$, valid for $\beta J < 1$, into $\hat\alpha = s(1-\beta J)$. The
bootstrap interval for $\hat\alpha$ excludes zero. That is not a result, for three reasons.
The outcome is a source-derived label, not a measured opinion. The fit performs worse than the
sample mean on held-out data, $R^2_{\text{held-out}} = -0.031$, so the relationship does not
generalise beyond the points used to estimate it. And $\hat\alpha$ is proportional to
$1-\beta J$, which nothing in this study measures, so any single value would be a statement
about a chosen $\beta J$ rather than about media.

The coupling is therefore \emph{unidentified}, which is a weaker claim than either a
detection or a null, and \textbf{no value of $\hat{\alpha}$ is quoted in this paper}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:freeenergy} shows instead how the free energy would respond at each category's
measured mean for a range of assumed couplings.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_free_energy_atlas.png}
\caption{Landau free energy $F(m)$ at $\beta J = 0.7$, evaluated at each category's measured
mean $X$ for assumed couplings $\alpha \in \{-1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1\}$. At this coupling the
model is below its critical point and each curve has a single minimum; the panels show how
little the category means tilt it. The sweep is not a fit: no value of $\alpha$ is estimated
from the data.}
\label{fig:freeenergy}
\end{figure}

The measurement does deliver the quantity Eq.~\eqref{eq:bound} consumes. Across the corpus
the observable attains
\begin{equation}
\Delta X = 0.1241 \qquad (\min X = -0.0698,\ \max X = +0.0543),
\end{equation}
and substituting into the companion paper's inequality with Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc} converts a
general statement into a concrete requirement (Table~\ref{tab:bound}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
$\beta J$ & $h_c(\beta J)$ & $\alpha$ required \\
\midrule
$1.102$ & $0.02101$ & $0.1693$ \\
$1.496$ & $0.20541$ & $1.6553$ \\
$2.000$ & $0.53284$ & $4.2938$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The field bound evaluated at three reduced couplings (grid points of the companion

This is a necessary condition and not a sufficient one. Clearing it does not establish that
media moves populations; falling below it excludes the mechanism within the model. Run B
gives a smaller spread, $\Delta X = 0.1176$, and hence $\alpha \ge 4.530$ at $\beta J = 2$.
Because a sample range is inflated by measurement error, the noise-free spread is no larger
than the measured one and the requirement it implies is no smaller, so the quoted values are
the lenient end.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What survives the confound}
\label{sec:whatsurvives}

It is worth separating the claims this study can defend from those it cannot, because the two
are easy to conflate.

\emph{Not supported.} That the instrument detects manipulation, or does so better than a
lexical model. That framing rather than provenance drives the separation. Any value for the
coupling. Any statement about an individual item. Any claim about subcortical structures, or
about measured rather than predicted activity.

\emph{Supported.} That the observable attains a measured spread of about $0.12$ on real
content, which is what the bound consumes and which does not depend on why the spread arises.
That the categories separate at an effect four times the detectable floor, and that the
separation replicates in an independent run. That high-outrage content, the category the
hypothesis favoured most, raises both networks together and so leaves the observable where
neutral content puts it. And that the coupling is unidentified on this data, which constrains
what a successor may assume.

The confound is removable by design rather than by analysis. Drawing all four categories from
a single source, so that provenance is held constant while framing varies, or crossing
sources with categories so that the two are statistically separable, would convert the
present result into an interpretable one. Neither requires new instrumentation.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Category is confounded with source dataset, and the \texttt{manipulative} label is
derived from category. This is the dominant limitation, stated in Section~\ref{sec:results}
as well as here. Category membership is also partly defined by selection rules (threat
keywords for fear-activating items, publisher-level labels for high-outrage items) rather
than by per-item annotation.
\item Single-run scores are noisy: $\mathrm{ICC}(2,1) = 0.8725$, with run-to-run standard
deviation $48.4\%$ of the between-item standard deviation. Category-level conclusions survive
this; per-item ones do not.
\item Predicted activation is not measured activation. Whether the released average-subject
checkpoint tracks measured cortical responses to synthesised speech of written text is not
tested here and is not established.
\item Cortical proxy only. The checkpoint cannot speak to subcortical structure.
\item Average-subject configuration, set by the loader; no individual differences are
represented.
\item Out-of-distribution stimuli: the encoder was trained on naturalistic audiovisual
material and is given synthesised speech from written text, with a synthesiser that may differ
between runs.
\item The parcel sets are the study's specification. The affective-salience set contains
orbitofrontal parcels and the deliberative-control set lateral orbitofrontal ones, and other
defensible choices would give a different observable.
\item $\Delta X$ is the range of one run on this corpus. It depends on corpus size and
composition, and differs between runs ($0.1241$ and $0.1176$).
\item The corpus is English-language from five specific sources, none of them African. A
search for openly licensed African news data carrying usable labels returned nothing
suitable, so the spread is a property of American and European publishing and the bound
inherits that limitation.
\item The field model is mean-field, single-population, static and binary.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Media enters mean-field opinion models as a field that is almost never measured. This paper
measured a candidate: an observable computed from content through a released cortical
encoder, applied to 400 items, with its spread, its reliability and its failure modes
reported rather than assumed. The instrument separates four corpora at an effect it was
powered to find, the separation replicates across runs, and it is confounded with provenance
in a way no analysis of this corpus can undo. A lexical baseline separates the same corpora
far better. The coupling the observable was built to calibrate is unidentified. What survives
is the spread, and through the companion paper's inequality a requirement,
$\alpha \ge 4.29$ at $\beta J = 2$, that any future proposal of this mechanism must meet
within all-to-all mean-field theory.
