\title{Bounding the external field in mean-field opinion dynamics: what any content
observable must satisfy to drive a transition}

\author[cuea]{Brian Mwai}
\ead{mwaibryn@gmail.com}
\address[cuea]{Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, The Catholic University of
Eastern Africa, P.O. Box 62157-00200, Nairobi, Kenya}

\begin{abstract}
Mean-field opinion models borrowed from the Ising universality class are routinely used to
argue that media content can tip a population between competing consensus states. In almost
every case the external field representing that content is assigned by hand. The models
therefore demonstrate that \emph{a} field of sufficient magnitude tips the population, while
saying nothing about whether any real content observable reaches that magnitude. We close
that gap from the theory side. Writing the field as $h = \alpha X$ for an arbitrary content
observable $X$ with attainable range $\Delta X$, we show that no media-driven transition is
possible unless the coupling satisfies $\alpha \ge h_c(\beta J)/\Delta X$, where
$h_c(\beta J)$ is the spinodal field at which the metastable minimum disappears. The bound
inverts the usual procedure: instead of fitting $\alpha$ and hoping the estimate clears zero,
it states what $\alpha$ would have to be for the mechanism to operate at all, and it can be
evaluated before any data are collected. We derive the Landau coefficients from the
self-consistency condition rather than asserting them, recover the mean-field exponents
$\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma = 1$ and $\delta = 3$ numerically as a check on the solver, and give a
screening criterion that any proposed field observable must pass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The statistical physics of social dynamics has a standard move: identify a binary attitude
with an Ising spin, identify social conformity with a ferromagnetic coupling $J$, identify
media or propaganda with an external field $h$, and study when the population's average
opinion $m$ jumps between consensus states \cite{castellano2009,galam2008}. The move is
productive. It supplies an order parameter, a control parameter and a transition, and it
explains why small changes in influence can produce disproportionate changes in collective
opinion near a critical point.

It also has a weakness that is rarely stated. The field $h$ enters as a free parameter. A
study fixes $h$ at whatever value makes the phenomenon visible, sweeps it, and reports that
the population tips at some $h^\ast$. The pattern is consistent across the literature that
models media explicitly: Crokidakis \cite{crokidakis2012} introduces mass media into the
two-dimensional Sznajd model as a probability $p$ of following the media opinion and sweeps
it, finding the phase transition absent above $p \approx 0.18$; Azhari and
Muslim \cite{azhari2023} apply an external field to majority-rule and $q$-voter dynamics and
sweep its magnitude; the review by S\^{i}rbu et al. \cite{sirbu2016} surveys models combining
peer interaction with external information without any of them measuring the external term.
In each case the swept parameter is the quantity whose real-world magnitude is at issue.

Nothing in such an exercise establishes that real media content produces a field anywhere
near $h^\ast$, because nothing in it connects $h$ to a measurable property of content. The
conclusion "a sufficiently strong field tips the population" is then a statement about the
model, not about media. The 2025 review of Ising-inspired sociophysics by Mullick and
Sen \cite{mullick2025} makes the same point from the other side, listing data-driven
calibration against large-scale social datasets among the field's open directions rather than
among its accomplishments.

One recent study breaks the pattern and is worth stating precisely, because it bounds what
this paper can claim. Korbel, Dahdoul and Thurner \cite{korbel2026} calibrate a double
random-field Ising model of elections directly to campaign expenditure and extract a critical
spending threshold from historical results, reporting it as the first time thermodynamic
parameters and a critical threshold have been taken from election data in this way. Their
field is measured, not assigned, which is exactly the move this paper argues for.

Two things nonetheless remain open, and they are what follows below. Their calibration is
fitted from outcome data after the fact, so it cannot screen a proposed mechanism before the
data exist; and it is specific to expenditure, whereas the observables proposed for media
\emph{content} remain uncalibrated. What is missing in both cases is a necessary condition
stated on the coupling itself.

This paper takes the missing step in the direction that does not require data. Suppose an
observable $X$ is proposed as the content-side quantity driving the field, through a linear
map $h = \alpha X$ with an unknown coupling $\alpha$. Suppose further, as is true of any real
observable, that $X$ has a bounded attainable range $\Delta X$ across the content a
population actually encounters. Then the requirement that the field be able to reach the
spinodal imposes a lower bound on $\alpha$ that involves no fitting:
\begin{equation}
\alpha \;\ge\; \frac{h_c(\beta J)}{\Delta X}.
\label{eq:bound-intro}
\end{equation}
Everything on the right is either a property of the model ($h_c$ as a function of the
coupling $\beta J$) or a property of the observable that can be measured cheaply
($\Delta X$). The bound is therefore a screening test: it can reject a proposed mechanism
before any expensive measurement of $\alpha$ is attempted, and it converts a failure to
detect a coupling into a quantitative statement about how large the coupling would have had
to be.

Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the model. Section~\ref{sec:landau} derives the Landau
coefficients from the self-consistency condition, correcting a form that does not follow from
it. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} validates the solver against the three exact mean-field
exponents. Section~\ref{sec:bound} derives the bound and Section~\ref{sec:screening} states
the screening criterion.

\section{Mean-field model}
\label{sec:model}

Take $N$ agents carrying binary opinions $s_i = \pm 1$, coupled all-to-all with strength
$J/N$, in an external field $h$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H} = -\frac{J}{N}\sum_{i<j} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i .
\end{equation}
The magnetisation $m = N^{-1}\sum_i s_i$ is the collective opinion polarisation, and in the
thermodynamic limit it obeys the self-consistency condition
\begin{equation}
m = \tanh\!\left(\beta J m + h\right),
\label{eq:selfcons}
\end{equation}
with $\beta = 1/k_B T$ and $k_B = 1$ throughout. We follow the convention that $h$ absorbs
the factor $\beta$, so $h$ is dimensionless and directly comparable to $\beta J$. The social
reading of $\beta$ is the inverse of a noise level: large $\beta$ describes a population that
copies its neighbours reliably, small $\beta$ one that decides close to at random.

Equation~\eqref{eq:selfcons} has a single root for $\beta J < 1$ at zero field and three for
$\beta J > 1$, of which the outer two are stable. The transition at $\beta J = 1$ is
continuous.

\section{Landau expansion with derived coefficients}
\label{sec:landau}

Write the free energy as a quartic in the order parameter,
\begin{equation}
F(m) = a\,m^2 + b\,m^4 - h\,m .
\label{eq:landau}
\end{equation}
The coefficients are not free. Requiring that $\partial F/\partial m = 0$ reproduce
\eqref{eq:selfcons} to cubic order fixes them. Inverting \eqref{eq:selfcons} gives
$\operatorname{artanh}(m) = \beta J m + h$, and expanding
$\operatorname{artanh}(m) = m + m^3/3 + O(m^5)$ yields
\begin{equation}
(1 - \beta J)\,m + \tfrac{1}{3} m^3 - h = 0 .
\label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
Matching term by term against $\partial F/\partial m = 2a m + 4b m^3 - h$ gives
\begin{equation}
a = \frac{1 - \beta J}{2}, \qquad b = \frac{1}{12}.
\label{eq:coefficients}
\end{equation}
Both follow from \eqref{eq:selfcons}; neither is chosen. This is worth stating explicitly
because coefficient forms that do \emph{not} follow from the accompanying self-consistency
condition appear in the applied literature, and the symptom is subtle: the free-energy curve
still has the right qualitative double well, so the figure looks correct, while its minimum
sits away from the $m^\ast$ that the same paper's root-finder reports. The double well opens
when $a < 0$, that is when $\beta J > 1$, recovering the transition already implied by
\eqref{eq:selfcons}.

Figure~\ref{fig:free-energy} shows \eqref{eq:landau} at zero field on both sides of the
critical coupling.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{F1_free_energy.pdf}
\caption{Free energy \eqref{eq:landau} at zero field for $\beta J = 0.8$, $1.0$ and $1.4$
with the coefficients \eqref{eq:coefficients}. The second minimum appears only above
$\beta J = 1$.}
\label{fig:free-energy}
\end{figure}

The linear response to the field is
\begin{equation}
\chi = \frac{\beta \operatorname{sech}^2(\beta J m^\ast + h)}
            {1 - \beta J \operatorname{sech}^2(\beta J m^\ast + h)},
\label{eq:chi}
\end{equation}
which diverges where the denominator vanishes and is undefined beyond it; we report $\chi$
only where the denominator is positive.

\section{Numerical validation against the exact exponents}
\label{sec:numerics}

Every later figure is drawn with the same solver, so the solver is checked first against
results that are known exactly. Mean-field theory fixes
\begin{equation}
\beta = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad \gamma = 1, \qquad \delta = 3,
\end{equation}
for $m^\ast \sim (\beta J - 1)^{\beta}$ at zero field, $\chi \sim |1 - \beta J|^{-\gamma}$,
and $m \sim h^{1/\delta}$ on the critical isotherm.

One methodological point matters here. Solving \eqref{eq:selfcons} by direct iteration of
$m_{k+1} = \tanh(\beta J m_k + h)$ is the natural implementation and is adequate away from
criticality, but its convergence degrades precisely at $\beta J = 1$, where the map's
derivative reaches unity. The exponents are defined in that limit, so fitting them to
iterated values measures truncation error rather than physics: doing so returns
$\delta \approx 1.35$ against an exact $3$. We therefore fit the exponents to roots obtained
by bracketing, and check the iterative solver against the bracketed one away from
criticality, where the largest absolute discrepancy over the sampled grid is
$\pbSolverAgreement$.

Fitting inside a window of width $\pbFitWindow$ in $|\beta J - 1|$ at $\pbFitPoints$ points
recovers
\begin{equation}
\beta = \pbBetaFitted, \qquad \gamma = \pbGammaFitted, \qquad \delta = \pbDeltaFitted,
\end{equation}
with absolute errors $\pbBetaError$, $\pbGammaError$ and $\pbDeltaError$ respectively. The
residual error in $\beta$ is the largest of the three and is a finite-window effect: the
power law is asymptotic, and the quartic term contaminates the slope as the window widens.
Figures~\ref{fig:order}, \ref{fig:chi} and \ref{fig:isotherm} show the three fits.

\section{The spinodal field and the bound}
\label{sec:bound}

Above $\beta J = 1$ the free energy has two minima at zero field. Raising the field deepens
one and shallows the other; at a finite field the shallower minimum disappears entirely. That
value is the spinodal field $h_c(\beta J)$, and it is the field a population in the
metastable state must experience before it is obliged to move. We locate it by bisection on
the presence of a second stationary point, which keeps it consistent with the solver used
elsewhere rather than introducing a closed form derived under different approximations.
Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows the resulting boundary; $h_c$ vanishes as $\beta J \to 1^+$ and
grows monotonically, reaching $\pbHcAtMax$ at $\beta J = \pbBetaJMax$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{F5_phase_diagram.pdf}
\caption{Phase diagram in $(\beta J, h)$. The shaded region is where two opinion states
coexist; its upper boundary is the spinodal $h_c(\beta J)$.}
\label{fig:phase}
\end{figure}

Now let the field be generated by a content observable. Any such proposal has the form
\begin{equation}
h = \alpha X,
\end{equation}
where $X$ is measurable on a piece of content and $\alpha$ is an unknown coupling carrying
the units. Real observables are bounded: across the content a population plausibly
encounters, $X$ spans some finite range $\Delta X$, which is an empirical property of the
observable and of the corpus, not of the model. The largest field the mechanism can deliver
is therefore $\alpha \Delta X$. Demanding that this reach the spinodal gives
\begin{equation}
\alpha \Delta X \;\ge\; h_c(\beta J)
\qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
\alpha \;\ge\; \frac{h_c(\beta J)}{\Delta X}.
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:alpha} plots the right-hand side against $\beta J$ for several values of
$\Delta X$. At $\beta J = \pbBetaJMax$ the required coupling is $\pbAlphaRequiredOneTenth$ for
an observable of spread $0.1$, $\pbAlphaRequiredHalf$ for spread $0.5$,
$\pbAlphaRequiredUnity$ for unit spread and $\pbAlphaRequiredDouble$ for spread $2$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{F6_alpha_required.pdf}
\caption{Coupling required before a content observable of spread $\Delta X$ can drive the
population across the spinodal.}
\label{fig:alpha}
\end{figure}

Three properties of \eqref{eq:bound} are worth drawing out.

First, it is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. Clearing the bound does not
establish that content tips populations; it only fails to rule it out. Falling below it,
however, is decisive within the model's assumptions.

Second, it does not require the field to be estimated. $\Delta X$ is obtained by measuring
the observable across a corpus, which is ordinarily far cheaper than estimating a coupling to
collective behaviour, and $h_c$ comes from the model.

Third, it turns a null result into a quantitative constraint. A study that measures $\alpha$
and obtains an interval containing zero has, on its own, only the statement that no coupling
was detected. Combined with \eqref{eq:bound}, the same interval says that the coupling would
have to exceed $h_c(\beta J)/\Delta X$ for the mechanism to work, and that the measurement
excludes that range at the stated confidence. This is a stronger and more useful claim, and
it is available to any study that reports a spread alongside its null.

\section{A screening criterion}
\label{sec:screening}

The bound supports a procedure that can be run before data collection.

\begin{enumerate}
\item State the observable $X$ and the map $h = \alpha X$ explicitly, including units.
\item Measure or bound $\Delta X$ on a modest sample of the content of interest. This does
not require the outcome variable and is usually cheap.
\item Choose the range of $\beta J$ the population plausibly occupies and read
$h_c(\beta J)$ from Figure~\ref{fig:phase}.
\item Evaluate $\alpha_{\min} = h_c(\beta J)/\Delta X$ and ask whether a coupling of that
size is defensible on independent grounds.
\item If it is not, the mechanism is excluded within the model and the expensive measurement
is unnecessary. If it is, proceed, and report $\Delta X$ alongside the estimate of $\alpha$
so that the bound can be applied to the result.
\end{enumerate}

The weakest link is step 4, since $\alpha$ has no independent scale in most applications. We
note that the criterion is still informative in relative terms: two candidate observables can
be ranked by $\alpha_{\min}$, and an observable whose spread is small enough to demand an
implausibly large coupling is a poor choice of field regardless of how well it correlates
with anything.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The result inherits every assumption of the mean-field model: all-to-all coupling, a single
homogeneous population, a static field, and a binary opinion. Network structure, heterogeneous
susceptibility and temporal dynamics all change $h_c$, and in general lower it, so
\eqref{eq:bound} should be read as a bound within this model rather than a universal one.
The linear map $h = \alpha X$ is the simplest possible coupling and is an assumption in its
own right; a saturating map would change the relationship between $\Delta X$ and the maximum
attainable field. Finally, the spinodal is the relevant threshold for a population trapped in
a metastable state; a population at the coexistence field can be moved by a smaller field
given enough time, so \eqref{eq:bound} is conservative in the sense of demanding more of
$\alpha$ than a nucleation argument would.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

All figures and every number quoted in this paper are produced by a single script,
\texttt{scripts/phase\_boundary.py}, which writes both the figures and a JSON record of the
values; the numbers in the text are generated from that record rather than transcribed. The
exponent check is enforced: the script exits non-zero if any fitted exponent departs from its
exact value by more than a stated tolerance, so a solver regression fails the run instead of
producing plausible curves.
